Why the envelopes mattered

Randomisation only protects a trial if the next allocation is unknowable at the moment a clinician decides whether to enrol someone. The list itself is random; concealment is what stops that randomness being undone at the bedside.

Permuted blocks keep the arms balanced throughout recruitment, which matters if the trial stops early or if patient mix drifts over time. The cost is predictability: within a block of fixed size, once enough allocations are known, the remainder is forced. With a block of 4 and two A's already opened, the last two lines must be B. Set the method to fixed blocks of 2 above and the predictable-positions metric climbs to half the list.

Random block sizes buy that predictability back down, which is why the block size — not just the seed — was kept off the site's copy of the protocol. Stratification runs a separate list per stratum so balance holds within each site, at the price of some imbalance overall, especially when strata are many and recruitment per stratum is small.

Reading the table

Each line is one sealed envelope. Allocation numbers are prefixed by stratum, as they were on paper, so a site could never take a line belonging to another site. Rows stay masked until opened — the unblinded view is what the statistician and pharmacy saw, and what the investigator never did.
